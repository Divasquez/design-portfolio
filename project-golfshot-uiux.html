Golfshot GPS
UI/UX Design

Work Summary

Golfshot is a mobile and smartwatch application created by Shortzoom Software that helps users play golf by providing GPS course maps, precise distances, hazard information, performance tracking, club recommendations, swing analysis, and personalized statistics powered by machine learning. It was founded in 2008 and has steadily grown in popularity since.

From 2017 to 2022, I worked at Shotzoom as a UI/UX designer, creating intuitive experiences across Golfshot’s ecosystem. I designed interfaces for iOS and Android smartphones, smartwatches, and the company’s primary website.

My work included modernizing original interfaces, designing new user experiences for upcoming features, and maintaining a cohesive design language across platforms. I collaborated with product managers and developers, incorporating direct feedback from usability testing and customer research.

Golfshot for Android: UI/UX Redesign

The Problem

The Android version of Golfshot had become outdated, with an aging interface and missing features compared to iOS.

Users frequently voiced their frustration through Google Play Store reviews and comments on social media.

The Solution

Redesigned Golfshot for Android with a modern, platform-native experience. The new design established its own visual identity while aligning with Android design patterns, introduced highly requested features, and built a scalable foundation for future updates.

The Result

The redesign delivered a more polished and competitive Android experience, leading to more app downloads, improved user satisfaction, and stronger customer retention.

This contributed to business growth through a larger user base and increased revenue.

Overview

Role: UI/UX Lead, Intern Manager

Led the redesign of Golfshot for Android over an initial 6-month design phase, partnering with an intern throughout the project. Worked with developers throughout the following year to implement, test, and iterate on the experience, ensuring the final product met both user and business goals.


Part 1: App Architecture

Using feedback from the iOS app as a guide, we reimagined the Android experience from the ground up. We prioritized the most used features, redesigned the home screen into a personalized hub, streamlined the “Start Round” flow, updated the core golf caddie experience, and mapped key user journeys.


System Audit & Analysis


What we learned from users

  • Golfers relied on a handful of core features and needed easy access.
  • Scoring navigation felt confusing.
  • Users wanted the app to feel more personal and relevant to how they play.

New Architectural Map


How it shaped the redesign

  • Reorganized navigation around the most frequently used features.
  • Simplified scoring user flows.
  • Created a personalized home screen with relevant content for each golfer.

Part 2: Visual Design

We redesigned the app’s key screens with a focus on clear information hierarchy, accessibility, and outdoor visibility. Through regular design reviews, we refined layouts and interactions, then validated them with interactive prototypes and user flows.


Part 3: Design System

We created a cohesive design system including reusable components, icons, buttons, color palettes, and a digital style guide. Usability testing helped refine the interface and ensure a consistent experience across the app.


Automatic Shot Tracking

The Problem

While shot tracking was already a feature in the app, it required players to remember to take out their phone and log the start and end of each shot.

However, this was burdensome, and players often forgot. Without this accurate tracking, users would miss out on two of the app’s best features—club recommendation and personalized statistics—and potential long-term benefits.

The Solution

By pairing the phone app with a smartwatch, such as an Apple Watch, we could train the watch to detect a swing. Each swing detected now acted as the logged start and end of each shot.

Machine learning was used to train the smartwatch program to detect the movement and differentiate between a practice swing and a real shot.

The Result

The feature worked wonderfully, allowing players a hands-free experience. They could focus on their game, knowing that their distances and statistics were being tracked accurately.

With accurate tracking, there are more accurate game statistics and individualized recommendations that can lead to users improving their golf scores and gaining the full value in the product.

Overview

Role: UI/UX Lead, Intern Manager

I worked closely with a team of developers on a feature that used machine learning, GPS, and complex calculations to automatically detect and track a golfer’s shots. Behind the scenes, there was a significant amount of technical work—from training the system to recognize swings to ensuring GPS distances and shot locations were accurate.

My role was to translate all of that complexity into a highly readable user experience across Apple Watch and iPhone.

For the Apple Watch I designed the experience to provide golfers with only the information they needed while playing. For the iPhone I designed a new Shot Editor interface that allowed golfers to review and manually adjust their recorded shots. This feature had been in development for several years, and after a direct collaboration with a team at Apple we successfully brought it to market.


Part 1: Watch Screens

The watch experience needed to be extremely simple. Golfers would be using it while playing, so the interface needed to communicate that shot tracking was active without distracting from the game.

I integrated the new tracking functionality into the existing watch app, using a simple Tracking indicator at the bottom of the screen along with a live distance counter.

As the golfer walked or drove toward their ball, the distance would update in real time. This gave users immediate visual confirmation that the feature was working while keeping the rest of the interface familiar and uncluttered.


Using already existing infrastructure and flows, I designed small additions to the interface that would clearly show that auto shot tracking was actively working.

On the watch screen, we prioritized having a clear indicator that demonstrated the feature in action, showing yardage dynamically changing as the player moved.

Part 2: Phone Shot Editor

The Shot Editor was a completely new feature designed for the phone and required a new set of controls and interactions.

We already had a 3D fairway view that allowed golfers to see all of their recorded shots. I used this existing architecture as the foundation for the editor, creating a dedicated editing screen where users could review and manually adjust individual shots.


Part 3: User Testing and Feedback

We released a beta version of the new features to a dedicated group of users. Their feedback became an important part of both the development and design process. User testing helped the developers improve shot detection. While I used the feedback to refine the interface and interactions.

One thing users particularly appreciated was being able to see the tracking feature working in real time on their watch. The Tracking indicator and live distance counter gave them confidence that their shots were being recorded accurately.

Feedback on the phone experience also helped me refine the Shot Editor, making the manual editing controls more intuitive and easier to use.


Part 4: Instructions and Promotion

Because this was a brand-new feature, we needed to communicate not only how it worked, but also why golfers would want to use it. Alongside the product UI, I created step-by-step instructions to help users understand the new functionality and how to get the most out of it.

Fortunately, the feature was adopted quickly, and customer support began receiving strong feedback from users.

I also created promotional and marketing materials, including social media advertising. One of the biggest highlights of the project was collaborating directly with a team at Apple to showcase the feature during the 2021 Apple Event.


Golfshot on Android Wearables

The Problem

With the success of Auto Shot Tracking for iOS and the ongoing updates for Android, our next challenge was bringing these experiences to Android-compatible wearables. We had users waiting AST and other features.

Designing for Android watches was complex, with two operating systems, Wear OS and Tizen OS, each with different capabilities, limitations, and a wide range of screen sizes compared to the line of Apple Watches.

The Solution

I designed for Wear OS and Tizen OS simultaneously, focusing first on core experiences like distances, scoring, and eventually Auto Shot Tracking.

We took an iterative approach, releasing features through beta testing and building on the experience as we gathered feedback from our most dedicated users.

The Result

In 2021, Tizen OS was discontinued, allowing the team to focus fully on Wear OS.

With that pivot, we were able to finally reach parity with iOS. This marked the completion of a years-long Android redesign, bringing our core product experience together across platforms.

A more complete and consistent Android experience helped us better serve a significant portion of our user base, strengthen the overall product, and create a stronger foundation for continued growth.

Overview

Role: UI/UX Lead

This project was a new challenge for me. I had never designed for Wear OS or Tizen OS, and there were almost no existing designs to build from. I was responsible for creating the wearable experience from the ground up, defining the UI, flows, and user journeys from scratch. My focus was bringing core Golfshot features—including distances, scoring, and eventually Auto Shot Tracking—to Android-compatible wearables.

Wearables were a natural extension of the Golfshot experience. Golfers spend hours outdoors, and constantly reaching for a phone can interrupt their game. A smartwatch could provide quick, hands-free access to important information when needed. This became a key principle throughout the project, every interaction needed to be quick, clear, and efficient, allowing users to stay focused on their game rather than their device.


Part 1: Understanding Smartwatches

I started by researching the Android wearable landscape—looking at the different devices, operating systems, screen sizes, input methods, and fitness apps already on the market. Through our support team, we also surveyed Android users to understand which devices they were actually using. We found particularly strong adoption of Tizen OS.


The biggest challenge was the variety of wearable. Some watches were almost entirely touchscreen-based, while others relied on physical buttons, rotating bezels, or dials. Rather than designing around a single device, I needed to identify interaction patterns that could work across the ecosystem.

One of my first design decisions was to prioritize glanceability and minimal interaction. A golfer shouldn’t have to stop what they’re doing to navigate through a complicated interface. Information needed to be immediately recognizable, and interactions needed to require as few steps as possible.

Step 2: Distances and Scoring

I started with the two most important experiences: distances and scoring. The goal wasn’t to recreate the phone experience on a smaller screen, so I had to rethink what information was actually necessary and how it should be presented.

For distances, I prioritized the information golfers needed most while playing: their yardage, the relevant target, and club recommendation. For scoring, I focused on reducing the number of interactions required to record a score. Since golfers are often walking between holes or preparing for their next shot, unnecessary navigation could quickly become frustrating.


Step 3: Auto Shot Tracking

Midway through this project the announcement about TizenOS being discontinued arrived. This shifted our priorities and allowed me to focus entirely on WearOS. At that point, the goal became bigger than simply adding features—we wanted the Android wearable experience to reach the same level of quality and functionality as iOS.

So once again I worked closely with developers to make Auto Shot Tracking work. We rolled out another beta test and got feedback on how to improve; this time, we focused on audio and haptic feedback. This was important to assure users the feature was working and they could have peace of mind.

Launching Auto Shot Tracking completed the Android redesign.
